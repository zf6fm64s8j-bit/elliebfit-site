# Above & Beyond Fitness LLC

## Arizona Liability Waiver Counsel Handoff

**Source reviewed:** `liability-waiver-draft-v2.pdf`  
**Business:** Above & Beyond Fitness LLC, Scottsdale, Arizona  
**Prepared:** August 16, 2026  
**Purpose:** Legal-risk review and drafting issues for Arizona counsel

> This memorandum is an informational drafting handoff, not legal advice and not a substitute for review by an Arizona-licensed attorney. The attached waiver should remain marked as a draft until counsel approves the final version.

## Executive recommendation

The draft is a credible starting point for adult clients and is unusually clear in identifying ordinary negligence. I would not approve it for use without revisions, however.

The most important conclusions are:

1. **Adult waiver:** Arizona may enforce a clearly worded release of ordinary negligence, but releases are strictly construed and enforceability may become fact-dependent.
2. **Minors:** Arizona does not appear to have a clear general rule authorizing a parent to waive a minor’s future ordinary-negligence claim in a commercial fitness agreement. The current minor provision should not be treated as reliable protection.
3. **Indemnity:** The third-party indemnity in Section 8 can be useful if limited to claims caused by the Client’s own conduct. The parent’s indemnity for claims brought by or on behalf of a minor is much riskier and may be viewed as an indirect release.
4. **Attorneys’ fees:** The prevailing-party clause is unlikely to guarantee recovery of fees for defending an ordinary negligence lawsuit. Remove it by default or narrow it to true contract-enforcement actions.
5. **Arbitration:** Do not add arbitration merely because a stray reference has been removed. It should be a deliberate business decision with a standalone, consumer-compliant clause. It does not cure weak waiver language or unresolved minor-authority issues.

## Key Arizona research conclusions

### Adult pre-injury releases

Arizona generally permits parties to release future ordinary negligence where no public-policy or statutory prohibition applies, but the language is strictly construed against the party relying on the release. See [Salt River Project v. Westinghouse](https://law.justia.com/cases/arizona/supreme-court/1984/17233-pr-2.html).

In [Benjamin v. Gear Roller Hockey](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/az-court-of-appeals/1486013.html), the Arizona Court of Appeals upheld an adult recreational release because it expressly covered the defendant’s own negligence, described the relevant activity, and was presented in a manner showing the participant understood the rights being surrendered.

The draft has those strengths because Section 2 expressly refers to ordinary negligence and Section 10 states that the Client is giving up the right to sue for ordinary negligence.

The risk is Section 3’s broad “any and all risks” assumption language. In [Phelps v. Firebird Raceway](https://law.justia.com/cases/arizona/supreme-court/2005/cv040114pr-1.html), the Arizona Supreme Court held that the validity of an express contractual assumption of risk is a question for the jury under [Arizona Constitution article XVIII, § 5](https://www.azleg.gov/const/18/5.htm). Phelps does not make every release invalid, but it reduces the likelihood of obtaining an early dismissal where scope, understanding, or applicability is disputed.

### Parental releases for minors

The Arizona answer is unsettled. I did not find a published Arizona appellate decision squarely deciding whether a parent may release a minor’s future ordinary-negligence claim in a commercial personal-training agreement.

In [Bothell v. Two Point Acres](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/az-court-of-appeals/1076951.html), a ten-year-old and her father signed a horse-program release. The court did not decide whether the parent had authority to waive the child’s claim; it resolved the case based on the scope of the release and expressly avoided the constitutional issue.

Arizona does expressly authorize a parent or guardian to sign a release in the limited context of equine activities. [A.R.S. § 12-553](https://www.azleg.gov/ars/12/00553.htm) does not apply to personal training, fitness instruction, virtual training, or PWR!Moves® services.

Arizona’s minor-claim rules also reflect strong judicial protection. Rule 16.3 directs courts to Rule 53, which generally requires judicial approval before a settlement of a minor’s personal-injury claim binds the minor. See the [Arizona Supreme Court’s Rule 16.3 order](https://www.azcourts.gov/Portals/0/20/2020%20Rules/Order-R200018.pdf?ver=69ztWUm2ohitibfWFMaQDw%3D%3D) and [Arizona Probate Rule 53](https://www.courtrules.net/arizona/az-probate/rule-53). Similarly, a conservator generally needs court approval to compromise or release a minor’s personal-injury or wrongful-death claim. See [A.R.S. § 14-5424](https://www.azleg.gov/ars/14/05424.htm).

These rules do not directly decide the validity of a pre-injury waiver, but they create a meaningful public-policy and litigation-risk concern. The safest operational approach is to use a separate minor-participation form that documents parental consent, health information, emergency procedures, and safety obligations without relying on a purported waiver of the minor’s substantive claim.

### Indemnity

A release attempts to prevent a claim. An indemnity generally shifts defense costs, settlements, or judgments after a claim is made. It does not prevent a minor or another claimant from suing Above & Beyond directly.

Arizona may enforce indemnity for an indemnitee’s own negligence only where the agreement expresses that intent clearly and unequivocally. See [Washington Elementary School District v. Baglino](https://law.justia.com/cases/arizona/supreme-court/1991/cv-91-0040-pr-2.html).

Section 8 is comparatively defensible because it is limited to third-party claims arising from the Client’s own negligence or breach. Section 11 is materially riskier because it attempts to make the parent indemnify Above & Beyond for “any claim brought by or on behalf of the minor” arising from ordinary negligence. That may be characterized as an indirect release or an attempt to shift the cost of Above & Beyond’s own negligence to a parent in a consumer/minor relationship.

Even if enforceable, a parent indemnity would not eliminate the minor’s claim. Its practical value would depend on the parent’s ability to pay and whether any insurance policy covers the contractual obligation.

### Prevailing-party attorneys’ fees

[A.R.S. § 12-341.01](https://www.azleg.gov/ars/12/00341-01.htm) permits a court to award reasonable fees in a contested action arising out of contract, but the award is discretionary.

Arizona courts have held that a personal-injury negligence action does not become a contract action merely because the defendant relies on a waiver. In [Benjamin](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/az-court-of-appeals/1486013.html), the court denied fees for defending the negligence action. [Dooley v. O’Brien](https://law.justia.com/cases/arizona/court-of-appeals-division-one-published/2010/cv090595.html) applied the same principle.

The current clause likely will not produce fee recovery simply because Above & Beyond defeats an injury claim. Its more plausible use is in a genuine action to enforce an express contractual obligation, such as an indemnity or payment obligation.

### Arbitration

Arizona generally enforces written arbitration agreements, subject to ordinary contract defenses. See [A.R.S. § 12-3006](https://www.azleg.gov/ars/12/03006.htm).

Arizona courts scrutinize arbitration clauses for excessive costs, one-sided obligations, inadequate notice, and terms that prevent a consumer from effectively pursuing a claim. See [Rizzio v. Surpass Senior Living](https://law.justia.com/cases/arizona/supreme-court/2021/cv-20-0058-pr.html) and [Clark v. Renaissance](https://law.justia.com/cases/arizona/court-of-appeals-division-one-published/2013/1-ca-cv-12-0692.html).

If arbitration is selected, use current AAA Consumer Rules or JAMS Consumer Standards, identify the administrator, allocate fees in a consumer-safe manner, preserve eligible small-claims rights, and provide a reasonable remote or Arizona forum. AAA’s current consumer information is available [here](https://www.adr.org/industries/consumer/); JAMS standards are available [here](https://www.jamsadr.com/consumer-minimum-standards).

Do not assume generic “heirs” or “representatives” language binds non-signatory wrongful-death beneficiaries. In [Dueñas v. Life Care Centers](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/az-court-of-appeals/1681330.html), the Arizona Court of Appeals held that statutory beneficiaries’ own wrongful-death claims were not subject to the decedent’s arbitration agreement.

## Draft-specific recommendations

### Sections 2–3: release and assumption of risk

- Replace “ordinary negligence or omission” with “ordinary negligence, including negligent acts or omissions.”
- Remove “however caused.”
- Tie the release to claims “arising out of or related to participation in the Services.”
- Replace “any and all risks” and “cannot be eliminated regardless of care” with “known and inherent risks” and “risks that may remain even when reasonable care is used.”
- Keep the exclusions for gross negligence, recklessness, intentional misconduct, and non-waivable liability, but add fraud and willful/wanton misconduct if counsel agrees.
- Limit property loss/theft language to losses caused by ordinary negligence and exclude intentional conduct.
- Change the reference to heirs, personal representatives, and assigns to “claims Client owns or has legal authority to release.”
- Consider a base adult waiver plus short service-specific addenda for outdoor, in-home, and virtual training.

Illustrative adult-release language:

```text
To the fullest extent permitted by Arizona law, Client releases and covenants not to sue Above & Beyond Fitness LLC and its owners, employees, trainers, independent contractors, and agents (the “Released Parties”) for Client’s claims for bodily injury, illness, death, or damage to Client’s personal property arising out of or related to Client’s participation in the Services, but only to the extent caused by a Released Party’s ordinary negligence, including a negligent act or omission.

This release does not apply to gross negligence, recklessness, willful or intentional misconduct, fraud, or liability that Arizona law does not permit to be released. It applies only to claims Client owns or has legal authority to release.
```

### Section 4: health screening and disclosure

The representation that the Client has no condition preventing safe participation is too absolute, particularly for clients with Parkinson’s disease or other chronic conditions.

Consider instead requiring the Client to represent that they have disclosed known conditions, medications, injuries, surgeries, pregnancy/postpartum information when relevant to safe programming, and other facts material to exercise. State that the form is not medical clearance and that Above & Beyond may require clearance, modify a session, or decline participation when reasonably necessary for safety.

Ensure the PAR-Q and health questionnaire are actually attached or separately delivered, dated, versioned, and retained with the signed waiver.

### Sections 6–7: environment and virtual training

Add operational requirements, not just liability language:

- Client provides a reasonably safe space and discloses known hazards.
- Client provides the current physical location for every virtual session.
- Client maintains a charged phone and access to emergency services.
- Client provides an emergency contact.
- Higher-risk clients may be required to have another adult present or obtain medical clearance.
- Above & Beyond may modify, postpone, or end a session due to heat, weather, unsafe conditions, equipment, symptoms, or technology limitations.
- The agreement should state whether Above & Beyond ever transports clients. If it does, transportation requires separate insurance and drafting review.

### Section 8: indemnity and fees

Keep the third-party indemnity concept, but add notice, defense-control, settlement, and “to the extent caused by” language. Do not use Section 8 to cover Above & Beyond’s own negligence unless counsel deliberately chooses that risk allocation.

If the fee clause is retained, limit it to a contested action solely to enforce an express contractual obligation and state that it does not create a fee entitlement merely because Above & Beyond prevails in a personal-injury or property-damage tort claim.

### Section 9: governing law and venue

- Add an exception for any forum required by applicable law.
- If arbitration is added, rewrite the venue clause to address court proceedings to compel, confirm, or vacate an award.
- Replace broad reformation language with severability or limitation “to the extent necessary.”
- Clarify that the waiver does not supersede separate payment, cancellation, refund, privacy, or service terms unless expressly intended.

### Section 10: acknowledgment

The explicit acknowledgment that the Client is giving up the right to sue for ordinary negligence is helpful. Consider adding that the Client:

- received a copy before participation;
- had a reasonable opportunity to ask questions;
- understands the agreement applies only to the services and parties identified; and
- understands there is no guarantee of a particular fitness, health, or Parkinson’s-related outcome.

Avoid stating that the agreement was “freely and voluntarily” signed if the waiver is mandatory and the Client had no realistic opportunity to negotiate it.

### Section 11: minors

Recommended conservative replacement:

```text
Parent/Guardian consents to Minor’s participation and represents that Parent/Guardian has legal authority to provide that consent. Parent/Guardian agrees to provide accurate health, emergency, and safety information and to comply with the applicable safety requirements.

Parent/Guardian releases only Parent/Guardian’s own claims, if any. Nothing in this section purports to release, waive, arbitrate, or limit any substantive claim belonging to Minor. Parent/Guardian will not be required to indemnify Above & Beyond for Above & Beyond’s own negligence.
```

If Above & Beyond elects to pursue a more aggressive parent waiver or indemnity, Arizona counsel should specifically evaluate parental authority, public policy, unconscionability, conflicts of interest, and insurance coverage. Require a new adult agreement when the minor turns 18.

### Photo/video consent

The optional, separate consent is a good structure. Add that withdrawal applies prospectively, that Above & Beyond will make reasonable efforts concerning future website use, and that it cannot control third-party reposts. For minors, use a separate parent marketing consent and do not combine it with the liability waiver.

## Optional arbitration language for counsel review

This is not ready-to-sign language, but it shows the intended structure if arbitration is selected:

```text
Except for eligible small-claims matters, disputes between Above & Beyond and a signing adult arising out of or relating to the Services or this Agreement will be resolved by binding, individual arbitration administered by the American Arbitration Association under its then-current Consumer Arbitration Rules.

The arbitration may be conducted remotely or at a reasonably convenient Arizona location. Above & Beyond will pay all administrator and arbitrator fees beyond the consumer court-equivalent filing fee. Each party will bear its own attorneys’ fees except to the extent an award is required or permitted by applicable law. The arbitrator may award any remedy available in court.

This provision does not purport to bind a non-signatory minor, heir, statutory beneficiary, or other person who did not agree to arbitrate.
```

If arbitration is added, counsel should also decide whether to include mediation, a class/collective-action provision, delegation of arbitrability, confidentiality, discovery limits, and special rules for minors.

## Practical pre-use checklist

- Decide whether minors will be accepted under this waiver at all.
- Decide whether the fee clause is worth its limited benefit.
- Decide whether arbitration is strategically desirable after accounting for administrator fees and consumer obligations.
- Confirm insurance for in-home, outdoor, virtual, contractor, minor, Parkinson’s-related, and transportation exposures.
- Create a separate emergency-contact and medical-emergency authorization.
- Attach and version the PAR-Q and health questionnaire.
- Preserve the signed version, delivery record, initials, date, and client identity.
- Train staff on heat, weather, equipment, emergency, virtual-session, and incident-reporting procedures.
- Obtain Arizona counsel’s approval before removing the draft notice or putting the form into use.
